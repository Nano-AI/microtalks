# Microtalks: animal characters and biome progression

Research and design recommendation · 22 September 2026

## Recommendation

Use fictional animal university students, preserving the existing identity mapping: `maya` → dog, `leo` → red fox, `sam` → owl. Organize eight lessons into four habitat-themed units. Each character can appear throughout the course; species should never determine difficulty or social rank.

The animal research below informs visual and behavioral inspiration. Character personalities, dialogue, lesson sequencing, and coaching rules are design proposals, not findings about human psychology or validated training outcomes. Sources are linked directly to the responsible wildlife, zoo, veterinary, and government organizations; these pages are institutional reference material rather than original experimental studies.

## What the animal evidence supports

| Animal | Documented behavior | Limit on the analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domestic dog | The [Merck Veterinary Manual][1] describes dogs as highly social, communicating through posture, facial expressions, vocalizations, and other signals. It explicitly describes variation in temperament and social signaling. [RSPCA guidance][2] distinguishes relaxed/playful behavior from worry and requests for distance; behavior also depends on personality and past experience. | An excited, curious student is a plausible fictional individual. Dogs are not uniformly outgoing, energetic, or endlessly available. A friendly appearance cannot establish willingness to interact. |
| Red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) | The [Woodland Trust][3] describes loose family groups, parental care, and young sometimes remaining to help raise later litters. Rural foxes tend to be warier of people; urban foxes may become accustomed to them. [The Wildlife Trusts][4] document use of grassland, woodland, and urban habitats, among others. | Wariness varies with circumstances. Habituation to people does not establish human-style trust, and these sources do not show a universal “shy → energetic with trust” sequence. Make that sequence optional and personal to Leo. Avoid the deceitful or manipulative “sly fox” stereotype. |
| Owl | [San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance][5] describes acute hearing, usually solitary habits, and vocal communication, especially during breeding. It also documents differences among owl species in habitat and activity. | “Owl” covers many species. These broad tendencies cannot establish one universal temperament. Hearing prey is not evidence of empathy; silent flight is not conversational quietness. Wisdom is a cultural association. Sam's thoughtful listening is authored characterization. |

Treat the owl design as a stylized owl unless a species is selected and separately checked. Across the cast, allow variation by individual, familiarity, current workload, and setting. No species label should act as a personality diagnosis.

## Character copy and conversation direction

### Maya · dog · `maya`

**Chooser copy:** “Maya is quick to say hello and curious about what you're into. She brings plenty of enthusiasm, and sometimes needs a moment to slow down and listen.”

- Opening: “Hey! Is this your first society fair too? I've already picked up far too many flyers.”
- Listening/repair: “Sorry, I jumped ahead. What were you going to say?”
- Boundary: “I'd like to hear more, but I've got a seminar in five minutes.”
- Practice: follow one topic, take a turn without matching her energy, and end warmly. Maya can be tired or serious. Her friendliness never obliges either student to disclose personal information or continue talking.

### Leo · red fox · `leo`

**Chooser copy:** “Leo likes a little time to settle into a conversation. Give him room and a shared interest, and you might meet his playful, animated side. Some days, a short chat suits him best.”

- Opening: “Hi. Is anyone sitting here? I'm waiting for the film society meeting.”
- Optional warmer turn: “You like practical effects too? I tried making a tiny spaceship last weekend. It went surprisingly badly.”
- Boundary: “I'd rather keep that private. Have you seen anything good lately?”
- Practice: offer an easy opening, share something small, and accept a brief answer. Familiarity can bring longer stories, but it never guarantees disclosure. A comfortable short exchange is a complete success; the learner is not tasked with making Leo outgoing.

### Sam · owl · `sam`

**Chooser copy:** “Sam often pauses before answering and enjoys a thoughtful question. They can get absorbed in a favorite topic, and appreciate being asked whether now is a good time.”

- Opening: “Hey. I'm taking a break from this reading. How's your afternoon going?”
- Interested turn: “Actually, that reminds me of a very strange campus-history story.”
- Boundary: “I need to finish this page first. Could we talk after class?”
- Practice: leave room for a reply, clarify ambiguous cues, and share without interrogating. Sam is a fellow student with imperfect knowledge, not an all-knowing tutor. A pause alone does not mean rejection or distress.

Shared authoring rule: all three have their own goals, can decline, and can misunderstand or repair. Retain ordinary university concerns and peer relationships. Animal movement is decorative expression; pair meaningful cues with dialogue or accessible scene text. Do not teach ear or tail positions as a code for interpreting real people.

## Four units, eight lessons

Use “biomes” as the product's map language. More precisely, these are habitat-themed settings: riversides and highlands are not single, uniform scientific biomes. Frame them as campus districts or university outing locations, with the same students visiting each.

| Unit | Lessons and campus setting | Practice goal and example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meadow · Start a conversation | 1. Open, at a welcome picnic. 2. Follow up, beside society stalls. | Use shared context and pick up one detail: “What got you interested in that society?” An acknowledged greeting or accepted decline can finish lesson 1 successfully. |
| Woodland · Find a shared pace | 3. Read the room, near a library garden. 4. Share, at an outdoor study table. | Check availability instead of guessing: “Good time for a quick chat?” Offer a small related experience and leave space for a response. |
| Riverside · Come and go | 5. Join, at a campus café terrace. 6. Exit, before heading to class. | Wait for an opening and accept the group's answer. Close directly: “Nice talking with you. I need to head to class.” Leaving needs no invented excuse. |
| Highlands · Handle a difficult moment | 7. De-escalate, during outing planning. 8. Support, after a disappointing presentation at a retreat. | Repair a mild misunderstanding: “That came out badly. Can I try again?” Ask about support: “Would you like to talk, or would some space help?” Accept either answer. |

The current README describes six guided lessons. Highlands proposes two additions; this report does not imply they already exist. Its late placement reflects combined skills and greater situational complexity. No character becomes a final boss.

### Why biomes beat a food-chain hierarchy

Biomes provide recognizable destinations and room for recurring relationships. Progress can represent practice in new situations without ranking students. Foxes already use several habitats [3][4]; owls also vary in habitat [5]. A fictional campus accommodates the domestic dog without pretending it has one wild biome.

A food chain offers an obvious ladder, but its meaning conflicts with this course. [NOAA][6] defines food webs through feeding relationships and dynamic predator–prey connections. Trophic position provides no measure of confidence, kindness, or conversational competence. Turning prey into beginners and predators into advanced partners would introduce a dominance narrative. Even dog social relationships resist a simple linear “pack hierarchy,” and Merck explicitly cautions against applying that account to dog–human interactions [1].

Use habitat completion markers and lesson names. Avoid predator ranks, taming language, and rewards for “winning over” a reluctant partner. The preference for biomes is a design judgment; the cited animal sources do not test educational effectiveness.

## Challenge tiers: fade support

Apply these tiers within every biome and with every character. Learners should be able to restore help.

| Tier | Support and situation | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guided | Stated goal, clear availability cue, optional sample replies, immediate coaching. | Learn a useful move with generous help. |
| Lightly guided | Keep the goal; put hints behind “Need an idea?” Include an ordinary pause or competing task. | Choose timing and wording with fewer prompts. Clarification remains a good answer. |
| Independent | Give a short scene brief, varied openings, and feedback after the exchange. Hints and replay remain available. | Combine skills and recover from a mild misunderstanding without step-by-step coaching. |

Difficulty must never mean stronger insults, humiliation, or increasingly hostile species. Keep interaction boundaries consistent across tiers. De-escalation permits setting a limit or leaving; the learner is not responsible for making an upset partner calm. Supporting someone permits listening or offering space without solving their problem.

Assess observable moves against the stated scene: a relevant follow-up, room for a reply, a clarification, or respect for a boundary. Cite the turn that supports the feedback. Do not award extra credit for longer speech, forced eye contact, faster replies, persistent questioning, or a partner becoming cheerful. Quietness is a valid conversational style. Avoid a hidden “trust meter” that rewards extracting personal details.

Suggested feedback: “You noticed Leo wanted to keep that private and changed the subject. That respected his boundary.” If the cue is ambiguous: “Sam's pause could mean several things. Checking whether they have time would be reasonable.”

## Conclusions

Adopt the dog/fox/owl mapping with the individual descriptions above. Use the four-unit habitat route, adding Highlands for de-escalation and support. Keep recurring characters flexible enough to surprise the learner without contradicting their expressed boundaries.

Before treating the design as effective training, try the copy and scenarios with university students, including quiet and neurodivergent participants. Check whether they understand that brief conversations and respectful exits count as success, whether cues invite clarification, and whether the animal styling feels appropriate for adults. This is the main unresolved product question; animal-behavior references cannot answer it.
